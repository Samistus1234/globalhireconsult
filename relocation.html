Moving Abroad: The Essential Checklist

A comprehensive pre-departure timeline covering documents, finances, housing, healthcare registration, and everything you need before boarding your flight.

10 min read 4 phases • 20+ action items
12

12 Weeks Before Departure

Documents

Gather and certify all essential documents: passport (valid for at least 12 months), nursing/medical degree certificates, professional registration certificates, marriage certificate (if applicable), birth certificates for dependents, police clearance certificates, and medical records including vaccination history.

Financial Planning

Open an international bank account (Wise, Revolut, or a global bank with presence in your destination country). Notify your current bank of your move. Research the currency and exchange rates in your destination. Establish an emergency fund covering at least 3 months of living expenses.

Licensing

Confirm your licensing status and any remaining requirements in your destination country. Ensure all exam results and registration documents are current and accessible.

Housing Research

Begin researching neighbourhoods, rental markets, and employer-provided housing options. If your employer provides accommodation, request details about the location, furnishings, and nearby amenities.

Health Insurance

Understand the healthcare system in your destination. Arrange travel insurance for the journey and interim health coverage until employer-provided insurance begins.

8

8 Weeks Before Departure

Visa Processing

Ensure your work visa application is submitted and track its progress. Have all required documents apostilled or authenticated as needed.

Accommodation

Secure temporary or permanent accommodation. If renting independently, consider short-term serviced apartments for your first month while you explore neighbourhoods.

Shipping & Logistics

Decide what to ship, store, or sell. Obtain quotes from international moving companies. Start decluttering and packing non-essential items.

Digital Preparation

Download essential apps for your destination (transportation, banking, translation, maps). Set up a VPN if needed. Ensure your phone is unlocked and research local SIM card options.

4

4 Weeks Before Departure

Final Administration

Close or redirect mail and subscriptions. Notify relevant authorities of your departure (tax office, voter registration, utility companies). Update your address with banks and insurance providers.

Medical Preparation

Schedule a comprehensive health check-up. Ensure vaccinations are up to date per destination requirements. Obtain copies of prescriptions and medical records. Carry essential medications with prescriptions in their original packaging.

Professional Preparation

Research your new workplace culture, uniform requirements, and first-day procedures. Connect with colleagues via social media or professional networks.

Farewell & Support

Spend quality time with family and friends. Establish regular communication schedules. Share your itinerary and emergency contacts.

Departure Day Essentials

Passport, visa, and work permit (physical & digital copies)
Original professional certificates & registration documents
Employment contract & employer contact details
Travel insurance documentation
Cash in destination currency ($300–$500 equivalent)
International debit/credit card
Phone with local SIM or international roaming activated
Essential medications & medical records
Laptop & important digital files backed up to cloud storage

What to Expect in Your First 90 Days

A week-by-week guide to your first three months abroad: workplace orientation, social integration, administrative tasks, and emotional adjustment milestones.

12 min read 4 phases • 12-week timeline

Week 1: Arrival & Orientation

Your first week will be a whirlwind of administrative tasks and new experiences. Prioritize completing mandatory onboarding requirements: HR paperwork, ID badge, IT system access, and workplace orientation.

Register with local authorities as required (e.g., council tax registration in the UK, Emirates ID in the UAE, Anmeldung in Germany). Open a local bank account — your employer's HR team can often recommend banks that are familiar with international workers.

Allow yourself to feel overwhelmed — it is normal. Jet lag, culture shock, and information overload are expected. Focus on essentials: sleep, food, and navigating your commute. Accept that you will not understand everything immediately.

Weeks 2–4: Building Foundations

Begin your clinical orientation and supernumerary period. Use this time to learn local healthcare protocols, documentation systems, medication names (generic and brand names vary by country), and communication norms. Ask questions freely — colleagues expect new international staff to need guidance.

Start establishing daily routines: grocery shopping, cooking, exercise, and connecting with fellow international colleagues. Join online groups or forums for healthcare professionals in your destination. Many hospitals have buddy systems pairing new international staff with experienced colleagues.

Weeks 4–8: Finding Your Rhythm

By now, you should be gaining confidence in your clinical role. Continue developing relationships with colleagues and patients. Start exploring your city beyond the immediate neighbourhood. Address any administrative tasks that were deferred: driver's licence conversion, utility setup, internet installation, and registering with a local doctor/dentist.

If you are experiencing homesickness, this is the period when it often peaks. Stay connected with home through video calls, but also invest in building your local social network. Join cultural associations, sports clubs, or faith communities. Many cities have active expatriate groups that organise social events.

Weeks 8–12: Settling In

You should now feel increasingly comfortable in your role and environment. Begin thinking about medium-term goals: career development plans, professional development opportunities, and personal milestones. Review your finances and ensure your savings plan is on track. Start exploring opportunities for professional growth within your organisation.

Reflect on your journey and celebrate your achievements. You have accomplished something remarkable: relocating to a new country, adapting to a new healthcare system, and building a new life. This resilience and adaptability will serve you throughout your international career.

Cultural Adjustment for Healthcare Workers

Understanding workplace culture differences, communication styles in clinical settings, and strategies for managing culture shock as a healthcare professional abroad.

9 min read 3 key topics

Understanding Workplace Culture Differences

Healthcare workplace cultures vary significantly across countries. In the UK and Australia, nursing practice emphasises autonomy, evidence-based care, and open communication with physicians. In Gulf countries, hierarchical structures may be more pronounced, and deference to senior staff is expected. In Germany, precision, punctuality, and formal communication are highly valued. In the US, patient advocacy and assertive communication are integral to nursing practice.

Understanding these differences before you arrive will help you navigate your new environment more effectively. Research your destination's healthcare culture, read blogs from international professionals who have made similar moves, and discuss expectations with your recruiter or employer.

Communication in Clinical Settings

Clinical communication encompasses far more than language proficiency. It includes understanding local medical terminology (which varies even between English-speaking countries), documentation standards, handover protocols, and the nuances of patient-provider communication. Patients may have different expectations about information sharing, family involvement in care decisions, and pain management approaches.

Practice active listening, ask for clarification when needed, and be transparent about any unfamiliarity. Most colleagues and patients appreciate honesty and a willingness to learn over false confidence.

Managing Culture Shock

Culture shock typically follows a predictable pattern. This cycle may take 6 to 12 months to complete:

1. Honeymoon — excitement & fascination 2. Frustration — irritation & homesickness 3. Adjustment — developing routines 4. Acceptance — feeling comfortable

Strategies for managing culture shock include maintaining connections with home while building local relationships, keeping a journal of positive experiences, exploring your new environment with curiosity rather than judgement, maintaining physical health through exercise and nutrition, and seeking support from peers who understand the experience.
